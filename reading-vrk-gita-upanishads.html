FOREWORD

The Philosophy of Advaita is based on the triple scriptural authority — the Upaniṣads, the Brahma-sūtra, and the Bhagavad-gītā — well-known in the tradition as prasthāna-traya. The Upaniṣads form the concluding portion of the Veda, which is śruti; and so they are called "Vedanta". There are quite a few Upaniṣads whose teachings have been organized and presented in the form of aphorisms in the Brahma-sūtra by Vyāsa. The Bhagavad-gītā which forms part of the Mahābhārata has the status of the Upaniṣads; and so it is as authoritative as the Upaniṣads. Śrī Śaṅkara at the beginning of his commentary on the Bhagavad-gītā observes that the famous Gītā- śāstra is an "epitome of the essentials of the entire Vedic teaching” (samasta-vedārtha-sāra-saṅgraha).

The central teaching of the Upaniṣads can be summarised in three propositions (1) Brahman is real; (2) the world is mithyā; and (3) the jīva in its essential nature is Brahman itself and not different from it. It will not be possible for one to understand the central teaching contained in these three propositions unless they are supported by doctrines and arguments. Though the Upaniṣads and the Bhagavad- gītā contain these doctrines and arguments, it will be difficult to detect them unless they are identified and set forth by one who is an adept in these texts. It is this most difficult task that has been accomplished in this monograph by Brahmaśrī V.R. Kalyanasundara Sastrigal. After identifying twenty doctrines contained in the Upaniṣads, he has also shown how each of these doctrines has its counterpart in the Bhagavad-gītā. Explanation of those doctrines has also been given.

There are different ways in which one can study a philosophical system. However, only an analytical study of a system will be profitable. One cannot think of a better introduction, simple, but scholarly, to an analytical study of the thought-structure of the Upaniṣads and the Bhagavad-gītā than the one presented by Śrī Kalyanasundara Sastrigal, in whom there is a happy combination of tradition and modernity. He has not only been teaching for nearly six decades prasthāna-traya and other Advaita classics, but also trained and guided research scholars, Indian as well as foreign, working in colleges and universities, through textual study and criticism.

I am happy to commend this book to scholars and laymen interested in Advaita as set forth in the Upaniṣads and the Bhagavad-gītā.

R. BALASUBRAMANIAN Director Sri Aurobindo School of Eastern & Western Thought Pondicherry University, Pondicherry 605 104

IntroductionThe Gītā and the Upaniṣads; the twenty doctrines

1SRI V.R. KALYANASUNDARA SASTRI

IN the sacred land of Bharat, Sri Vedavyāsa is considered to be an avatār manifesting the divine aspects of Brahmā, Viṣṇu, and Maheśvara.1 The Vedas which had got mixed up before his avatār were classified by him into four as we have them today. With a view to making the valuable knowledge contained in the Vedas available to the people at large in a manner easily understandable, he composed the great Purāṇas and the Upapurāṇas. Subsequently he composed the celebrated Mahābhārata which is the quintessence of Vedic and Purāṇic thought.2

The different values which man pursues can be grouped under four heads, viz., Righteousness (dharma), Wealth (artha), Pleasure (kāma) and Liberation (mokṣa). We have the most elaborate and scholarly treatment of these values in the Mahābhārata. The analysis of these values is comprehensive and conclusive— so comprehensive that whatever view is expressed in other works is not found missing in the Mahābhārata and whatever has not been stated in it is not found in any other treatise, and so conlusive that its final position is accepted as the most authoritative and the most binding, because of the logical basis on which it stands.3 It is, therefore, no wonder that in the tradition of learned folk, the Mahābhārata has been justly praised as the fifth Veda. The Upaniṣads which form a part of the Vedas deal with the knowledge of the ultimate reality called Brahman or Ātman. In the same way the Bhagavadgītā which forms a part of the Mahābhārata is an enquiry into the nature of the ultimate reality, viz., Brahman or Ātman. The Gītā is related to the Mahābhārata in the same way as the Upaniṣads are related to the Vedas. The Gītā has been rightly characterised as the Upaniṣad of the Mahābhārata. Since the Mahābhārata contains the Gītā which has the status of the Upaniṣad, It is called the fifth Veda. The significant expression Bhagavadgītāsu upaniṣatsu used by the learned ones clearly brings out the idea that the Gītā has the supreme status

2of an Upaniṣad. The great Śaṅkara has observed that the Gītā has, in a condensed form, the essence of the Vedas.4 Ānandagiri, the celebrated commentator, points out that the Gītā is of the nature of an elaborate annotation on the mahāvākyas and contains three kāṇḍas (Mahāvākya vivaraṇātmikā kāṇḍatrayātmikā gītā).

What are known as the principal texts (mahāvākyas) are those which teach the non-difference of the jīva and Brahman. the Gītā is of the nature of a commentary on the significance of the principal texts. According to Ānandagiri's classification, the first six chapters of the Gītā constitute the first part known as Jīva-kāṇḍa, the next six chapters constitute the second part called Brahma-kāṇḍa and the last six chapters constitute the third part known as Aikya-kāṇḍa.5

The word 'Upaniṣad' means Brahma-vidyā or Ātma-vidyā, i.e., the knowledge of Brahman or Ātman. That part of the Vedas which deals with and conveys the knowledge of Brahman or Ātman is therefore called Upaniṣad. Śaṅkara brings out this point in his commentary on the Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upaniṣad.6 In other works too, particularly in the beginning of his commentary on the Kaṭhopaniṣad, Śaṅkara lays emphasis on this point. In the light of this interpretation, the Gītā too which deals with Brahma-vidyā deserves to be characterised as an Upaniṣad. Just as the Upaniṣads are the utterance of the Lord, so is the Gītā the utterance of the Lord.7

The jīva can escape from saṁsāra which is bondage and attain to liberation only on the attainment of the knowledge of Brahman. There is no other means by which bondage can be put an end to. The one and only aim of the Upaniṣads is to convey the saving knowledge of Brahman and the means by which such a knowledge can be obtained. Many passages from the Upaniṣads can be cited in support of this view.8

6It is not difficult to show that the Upaniṣads and the Gītā have the same end in view. The first chapter of the Gītā which describes the grief of Arjuna is called Arjuna-viṣāda-yoga. In the verses from 'dṛṣṭvā tu Pāṇḍavānīkam (I-2) to 'na yotsya iti Govindamuktvā tūṣṇīm babhūva ha' (II- 9) there is a graphic description of the sense of egoity characteristic of Arjuna, and of the grief and sorrow, delusion and disappointment which follow in its wake, making him a pitiable object caught in the slough of samsara.9 Arjuna is portrayed as a representative of mankind caught in the life-cycle. The teaching of the Gītā is intended to remove the delusion and despondency of Arjuna and those of others who are in the same predicament, by an unravelling of their cause. Ignorance of the nondual Brahman which is no other than the innermost Self of the individual, is the source of all trouble. And when once the knowledge of Brahman is attained, ignorance, (avidyā), and its effects disappear.

The teaching of the Gītā has its effect on Arjuna. It serves the purpose for which it is intended. Arjuna declares (XVIII-73) that he is no more the victim of doubts and delusions and that he has attained the true nature of the Self through the grace of the Lord.11 The passages quoted from the Swārājyasiddhi emphasise the same ideas as the Gītā does. Sankara's emphasis on the same truth is contained in the verse extracted from Anātma Sri-Vigarhaṇam. The following verse from Vedavyāsa is to the same point. We see therefore that in a general way the Upaniṣads and the Gītā have the same end in view.

9In the Gītā we come across a number of Upaniṣadic texts repeated verbatim or with slight modifications. We furnish below identical or near-identical passages taken from the Upaniṣads and the Gītā.15 It can also be proved that the Gītā emphasises in

10unmistakable terms the central thesis of the Upaniṣads. According to the Upaniṣads, Brahman which is the ultimate reality is eternal and ever existent. The pluralistic world we are familiar with is an illusory appearance (mithyā). This appearance has for its substratum Brahman, and is the result of ignorance about Brahman. When the knowledge of Brahman dawns, this appearance caused by ignorance disappears. The jīva is, in essence, non-different from Brahman. It is Brahman which is seated in every jīva as its innermost being. It is by realising the non-difference between Brahman and the jīva that release (mokṣa) can be attained. This, in short, is the central philosophy of the Upaniṣads. The verses quoted below 1616 sum up the central teaching.

A careful study of the Upaniṣads makes it clear that this central thesis is sought to be established with the help of a number of arguments and assertions which are inter-connected. We list below twenty basic arguments and assertions:

1. Sentient being is the cause of the universe.

2. The entire universe is Brahman.

3. The world is an illusory appearance of Brahman.

4. The doctrine of Māyā.

5. Twofold knowledge.

6. Brahman is non-different from jīva.

7. Jīva is no other than Brahman.

8. Release is possible only through the knowledge of non-difference between jīva and Brahman.

9. Karmas are indirect means.

10. Calmness of mind, self-control, etc., are direct means.

11. Brahman-knowledge destroys karma.

12. Karmas do not bind a realised person.

13. The superiority of the fruit of knowledge.

14. The knowledge of the non-dual Truth cannot be attained all too easily.

15. Brahman-knowledge has to be attained in this life itself.

16. Liberation is an eternal state.

17. Fruits got through Karma are ephemeral.

18. Those who are in bondage revolve in two different paths.

19. The excellence of meditation on Praṇava.

20. The last thoughts determine the next birth.

29Gold and other materials are capable of transformation into the various objects made of them. After the transformation they undergo, they appear as different objects—clay as pot, pan, etc., and gold as bangles, bracelets, etc. The point of comparison between Brahman and these materials is that Brahman appears as the world of diversity in the same way as gold appears as the different ornaments made of it. The comparison must not be stretched too far, for there is all the difference between gold appearing as the different ornaments made of it and Brahman appearing as the world of diversity. It is, therefore, necessary to ascertain the point implicit in the texts cited above without being carried away by a superficial understanding of them. The change which clay, gold, etc., undergo in the process of becoming objects made of them, is known as transformation (pariṇāma).1 Such a transformation is neither possible nor intelligible in the case of Brahman. When we say that Brahman appears as the world of diversity, it is not a case of transformation but of transfiguration or illusory appearance (vivarta), Brahman transfigured as the world of diversity. Just as a rope whose real nature is not, for the time being, known, illusorily appears as a snake, just as a nacre whose real nature is not known, illusorily appears as silver, so also Brahman, due to our ignorance (avidyā) of its real nature, illusorily appears as the world of diversity.2 In the case of the rope-snake or the nacre-silver illusion, there is no actual transformation of the one into the other; in the

30same way in respect of Brahman and the world, there is no actual transformation of the former into the latter. In short, the theory of vivarta as distinguished from the theory of pariṇāma is fundamental to the system of Advaita which the Upaniṣads expound. The following texts from the Upaniṣads elucidate the point: (a) 'All this- whatsoever moves on the earth-should be covered by the Lord. Protect (your Self) through that detachment. Do not covet anybody's wealth'. (b) 'He who sees all beings in the very Self, and the Self in all beings feels no hatred by virtue of that realisation'. (c) 'When to the man of realisation all beings become the very self, then what delusion and what sorrow can there be for that seer of oneness?'. (d) 'Have you, my dear, ever asked for that instruction by which one hears what cannot be heard, by which one perceives what cannot be perceived, by which one knows what cannot be known?'. (e) 'When the Self, my dear, is realised by being heard of, reflected on and meditated upon, all this is known'. (f) 'Indeed there is nothing to be known beyond this'. (g) 'It should be known that energy assumes various forms such as earth, water, light, air and ether'. (h) 'From whom this universe proceeds in the form of cycles'. (i) 'In this Self are fixed all gods, all worlds, all organs and all these selves'. (j) 'That self is indeed Brahman, as well as identified with the Intellect and the Manas'. 33

The same idea is conveyed by the following passages from the Gītā (a) 'By Me who is Unmanifest Form, is all this world pervaded. All beings dwell in Me; and I do not dwell in them'. (b) 'Knowing which nothing more besides remains to be known here'. (c) 'He who sees Me everywhere and sees everything in Me, to him I vanish not, nor to Me does he vanish'. (d) 'The Self abiding in all beings and all beings abiding in the Self, sees he whose self has been made steadfast by Yoga, who everywhere sees the same'. (e) 'As the mighty wind moving everywhere rests ever in the ākāśa, so know thou, do all beings rest in Me'. (f) 'As the all-pervading ākāśa is, from subtlety, never soiled, so the Self seated in the body everywhere is not soiled'.4

It may be pointed out in this connection, that according to Vidyāraṇya the change which takes place when clay, gold etc., appear as different objects made of them has to be characterised as vivarta. The objects made of clay, gold, etc., on disintegration re-assume the form of their cause. The effects in the forms of the different

31objects ceasing to exist, the cause alone remains. This is the characteristic feature of vivarta-vāda. Vidyāraṇya grants that the change as a result of which milk becomes curd is, strictly speaking, pariṇāma. Here the effect can never re-assume the form of the cause.5

4The doctrine of māyāThe doctrine of Māyā.A · What is real
word by wordमायाmāyāthe power that projects the appearance

34The doctrine of māyā becomes necessary in order to establish the theory of vivarta which has been referred to above. Māyā which is insentient (jaḍa) and indeterminable is composed of three guṇas viz., sattva, rajas and tamas. It is otherwise known as avidyā or ajñāna. It has two powers — the power of concealment and that of projection. By the one, it suppresses the truth, and by the other, it suggests the false. By suppressing the real nature of the non-dual Brahman, it projects the illusory appearance of the world of plurality. Therefore, the world-appearance which is due to māyā or avidyā is not real. The experience of world-appearance persists so long as māyā continues its play; and when māyā or avidyā is removed, the worldappearance, too, disappears. This, in short, is the main idea of the doctrine of māyā.

The following Upaniṣadic texts lay emphasis on the work of māyā.

(a) ‘There is one un-originated Principle of red, white and black colours which gives birth to numerous offsprings like itself. An unborn (individual soul) becomes attached to it and enjoys it, while

35another unborn (the Puruṣa or Spirit that has liberated itself from the clutches of Prakṛit) leaves it having enjoyed it'. (b) 'The sages, absorbed in meditation through concentration of mind, discovered the creative power belonging to the Lord Himself and concealed in its own guṇas. The non-dual Lord rules over all those causes-time, the Self and the rest'. (c) 'The Vedas speak of His exalted power, which is innate and capable of producing diverse effects, and also of His omniscience and might'. (d) 'There was neither Aught nor Naught. There was darkness. In the beginning all this was hidden in darkness'. (e) 'These same are the true desires covered by the untrue'. (f) 'In the end, again (one attains) the cessation of the illusion of phenomena'. (g) 'The one unborn prakṛit which brings about the relation of the enjoyer, enjoyment and the object.’1

The same point is emphasised in many places in the Gītā. (a) 'Verily, this divine illusion of mine, made up of guṇas, is hard to surmount'. (b) 'I am not manifest to all, veiled (as I am) by Yoga- māyā'. (c) 'Evil-doers and the deluded and the vilest among men, deprived of knowledge by māyā, and following the ways of the asuras, do not worship Me'. (d) 'Knowledge is veiled by ignorance, and thereby mortals are deluded'. (e) 'Deluded by these threefold guṇas constituting Nature, this whole world fails to recognise Me, who am above the guṇas and immutable’.2

Śankara brings out the nature of māyā or avidyā in one verse as follows: 'She is neither existent nor non-existent nor partaking of both characteristics; neither same nor different nor both; neither composed of parts nor an indivisible whole nor both. She is most wonderful and cannot be described in words.’3

The same idea is stressed in the following verse in Swārājya Siddhi: ‘ Brahman is bereft of any modification; still it creates the world; but not without the help of māyā and this māyā too is illusory in as much as the signification of that which is illusory, is well-known in the world by the word māyā. And also because the Śrutis declare its absolute cessation on the attainment of the knowledge of the Supreme Brahman. This māyā indeed is avidyā. Though it is of illusory nature, it is well-known in the world that with its help everything is accomplished. Therefore, this phenomenal world which is an illusory appearance of Brahman, who is of the nature of pure consciousness, is inexplicable even as the colour of the feathers of

36a peacock is inexplicable (on account of admixture of a variety of colours).'44

5VidyādvaividhyamTwofold knowledge.A · What is real
word by wordविद्याvidyāknowledgeद्वैविध्यम्dvaividhyamtwofoldness

38A. Knowledge is of two kinds: (1) parāvidyā and (2) aparāvidyā. By parā-vidyā is meant the knowledge of Brahman or Atman. It is the knowledge which gives rise to liberation (mokṣa) which is the supreme end of human endeavour. Any knowledge other than this is aparā-vidyā and is the cause of bondage. Dharma, artha, and kāma are values which we cherish and pursue; but they will not confer mokṣa or liberation. And any amount of knowledge one may acquire about these is aparā-vidyā which is referred to as avidyā in the Upaniṣads. Again in the Upaniṣads, liberation is referred to by the term 'śreyas' and bondage by the term 'preyas'. In short, the knowledge which is conducive to the realisation of the non-dual Self is parā-vidyā, and that which keeps us in the state of bondage is aparāvidyā. That man must endeavour to attain parā-vidyā, i.e., the

39knowledge of Brahman, by overcoming, aparā-vidyā which is the cause of bondage, is the basic lesson which the Upaniṣads seek to convey in different ways.

The Upaniṣadic texts cited below emphasize this lesson: (a) 'There are two kinds of knowledge to be acquired-the higher and the lower. This is what, as tradition runs, the knowers of the import of the Vedas say. Of these the lower consists of the Ṛg-Veda, Yajur-Veda, Sāma-Veda, Atharva-Veda, the science of pronunciation, etc., the code of rituals, grammar, etymology, metre and astrology. Then there is the higher knowledge by which that Immutable Principle is realised'. (b) 'That which is known as knowledge and that which is known as ignorance are widely contradictory, and they follow divergent courses. I consider you, Naciketa, to be an aspirant for knowledge, in as much as the desirable things, multifarious though they may be, did not tempt you'. (c) 'O Naciketa! You, such as you are, have discarded, after consideration, all the desires that are themselves delightful or are the producers of delight. You have not accepted this path of wealth in which many a man comes to grief'. (d) 'The preferable is different indeed; and so, indeed, is the pleasurable different. These two, serving divergent purposes, bind men. Good befalls him who accepts the preferable between these two. He who selects the pleasurable falls from the true end'. (e) 'They say that by vidyā a really different result is achieved, and they say that by avidya avidyā different result is achieved. Thus, we have heard from those wise men who explained that to us'. (f) 'After departing from this world, does any ignorant man go to the other world (or not)? Alternately, does any man of knowledge, after departing from this world, reach the other world (or not)?' (g) 'Those who depart from here without having realised the Atman: and those who depart from this world, having understood the Ātman'. (h) 'The man who desires; but the man who does not desire'11

The same lesson is conveyed by the following passages from the Gītā: (a) 'Constancy in the knowledge of the Self, and an insight into the object of the knowledge of Truth, this is declared to be knowledge, and all that is contrary to it is ignorance'. (b) 'It is the Sovereign Science, the Sovereign Mystery, the Supreme Purifier. It is perceived by immediate experience, it accords with Dharma, it is easy to practise, and is imperishable'. (c) 'Of all sciences, I am the

The ever-existent, non-dual Brahman is seated in all creatures (from the Creator to a clump of grass) which are in the state of māyā. 54The Jīvas appear to be different from one another only on account of the limiting adjuncts, viz., the body, senses, etc. In truth, all the jīvas are no other than Brahman, which is one and non-dual. 57It is through the realization of Brahman as being identical with jīva and of jīva as being non-different from ātman, as indicated earlier, that a person attains liberation. There is no other way to attain it. One who percetives difference between Brahman and jīva is the victim of fear and is subject to the cycle of birth and death. The highest that a person can think of for the purpose of realisation is the knowledge of the non-dual truth, viz., Brahman or Ātman. Such a realisation is release (mokṣa) which is bliss par excellence, without any trace of sorrow.

The Upaniṣadic texts given below emphasise this point: (a) If a man realises the Self as 'I am this'. (b) This (self) was indeed Brahman in the beginning. It knew only Itself as, 'I am Brahman'. (f) Only when men shall roll up the sky like a skin, will there be an end of misery for them without realising God. (g) With the knowledge of God, all fetters are destroyed. 62Karma which is enjoined in the Vedas is of three kinds: (1) that which is to be done through the physical body (kāyikam), (2) that which is to be done through utterance of words (vācikam) and (3) that which is to be done mentally (mānasam). 65The Upaniṣads declare that six disciplines like the control of the senses, control of mind, etc., are indispensable for attaining Brahman knowledge. By a little elaboration we can extend the number of disciplines which are conducive to Self-realisation to eight. They are as follows: (a) Discrimination between things permanent and things transient: (b) Renunciation of the enjoyment of fruits of action, in this world and in the next; (c) Practice of calmness of mind, self-

66control etc.; (d) The intense desire to attain liberation; (e) Hearing of the Text (Śravaṇa); (f) Critical inquiry into it (manana); (g) Repeated contemplation of it (nididhyāsana); and (h) Understanding the meaning of the words 'That' (Tat) and 'thou' (tvaṁ). 72Karmas performed after self-realization do not bind a realised soul.

When a person continus to live in his physical body even after attaining the immediate knowledge of Brahman, no action performed in the course of his life can bind him. Strictly speaking, whatever he does is not karma at all in the usual sense in which it is used.

The Upaniṣadic texts given below set forth this view: (a) Just as water does not cling to the lotus leaf, so also sin does not cling to him who knows Brahman thus. (b) This dyke, (i.e, the Ātman) neither the day nor the night crosses, nor old age nor death nor sorrow, nor merit nor demerit. (c) He conquers both of them. Things done or not done do not trouble him.1

The following passages in the Gītā also support the same idea: (a) He who works without attachment, resigning his actions to Brahman, is untainted by sin, as a lotus-leaf by water. (b) Regarding alike pleasure and pain, gain and loss, success and defeat, prepare yourself for battle. Thus you will incur no sin.2

72The superiority of the fruit of knowledge.

Whatever fruits are obtained either through means which are empirically well-established or through karmas and upāsanas recommended and / or enjoined by scriptures, (whether these are considered individually or in their entirety) are included in the fruit of knowledge. Rightly speaking, they are inferior to the fruit which is obtained through the knowledge of Brahman. It is, therefore, advisable that without caring for fruits which are limited, inferior, and ephemeral, one must endeavour to attain Brahman knowledge which assures us of the highest bliss that is ever-lasting and eternal. 75Nothing is more difficult to attain than the knowledge of the Nondual Brahman or Ātman. The Upaniṣads declare that the Ātman is subtler than the subtlest, and that it is devoid of form and qualities. Hence it cannot be known through the senses in the way the other objects are known. Nor can it be known through the intellect. Even the Devas find it difficult to attain such a knowledge as will reveal the Ātman. Not however that it cannot be known. The idea is that it is not just one among the many objects which we claim to know in our day to day experience. It can be known only through ceaseless effort with an intense longing for it. 82Many conflicting views are held with regard to the nature of liberation. According to the Upaniṣads, liberation consists in the realisation of the tri nature of the Self. Owing to the influence of the beginningless avidyā, the eternal, non-dual Self which is not related to the body, the senses, etc., is thought of in association with them and as limited by them. Realisation of the true nature of the Self as eternal and unlimited, ever-free and never-bound, is, according to the Upaniṣads, liberation. It is a state of incomparable beatitude without even the slightest trace of sorrow. There is no rebirth to one who remains in one's own state, i.e., to one who realises the true nature of the Self.

The following śruti passages drive home this point: (a) Then a mortal becomes immortal and one attains Brahman here; (b) He, however, who has understanding, who has control over his mind and is ever pure, reaches that goal from which he is not born again. (c) May I never go to the white, toothless, to the toothless, white, devouring; may I never go to it. (d) One's deeds and the self, consisting of understanding, all become one in the Supreme Immutable Being. (e) He attains supreme equality with the Lord. (f) His vital breaths do not move up. They are gathered together here itself. (g) Being Brahman he goes to Brahman. (h) He never returns hither again. (i) The work of him, who meditates on the Self alone as his world, is not exhausted.1

20AntyavijñānamThe last thoughts determine the next birth.D · The path of those still bound
word by wordअन्त्यantyalastविज्ञानम्vijñānamcognition, thought

97The next birth of a person will be in accordance with the last thoughts he entertains on the eve of his death. It will be possible for a person to have good thoughts in the last stage of his life only if he has been thinking good thoughts earlier. It is for this reason that we are exhorted to entertain only good thoughts, which when done deliberately over a long period of time becomes a habit and a life-long acquisition. An insect which creeps on a blade of grass leaves it off only after getting hold of another blade. In the same way, the jīva leaves off the mortal coil only after getting hold of

98another life. In other words, every jīva leaves off its embodied existence only with a fore-knowledge of its future life. This knowledge is known as aṅtya-vijñāna.

The following Upaniṣadic texts convey this idea. (a) The top of his heart brightens. Through that brightened top the self departs. (b) The self has a particular consciousness, and goes to the body which is related to that consciousness. It is followed by past knowledge, work and experience. (c) Together with whatever thought he had (at the time of death), a person enters into Prāṇa. Prāṇa in combination with Udāna and in association with the soul, leads him to the world desired by him. (d) Now, verily, a man consists of will. As he wills in this world, so does he become when he has departed hence. Let him (with this knowledge in mind) form his will. (e) Just as a leech supported on a straw goes to the end of it, takes hold of another support and contracts itself, so does the self throw this body aside, —makes it senseless—takes hold of another support, and contracts itself.1

The same truth is brought out in the following passages in the Gītā: (a) For whatever object a man thinks of at the final moment, when he leaves his body—that alone does he attain, O son of Kunti, being ever absorbed in the thought thereof. (b) Therefore, at all times, constantly remember Me and fight. With your mind and understanding absorbed in Me, you will surely come to Me. (c) I am easy of access to that ever steadfast Yogi who, O Pārtha, constantly meditates on Me and gives no thought to anything else. (d) And whoso, at the time of death, leaves his body remembering Me alone and goes forth—he attains My Being; concerning this there is no doubt. (e) O Pārtha, there is no destruction for him either in this world or the next; no evil, My son, befalls a man who does good. (f) The man who has fallen away from Yoga goes to the worlds of the righteous. Having lived there for unnumbered years, he is reborn in the abode of the pure and the prosperous. (g) Or he is born in a family of Yogis rich is wisdom. Verily, such a birth is hard to gain in this world. (h) There he comes in touch with the knowledge acquired in his former body. By that former practice alone he is led on in spite of himself. Even he who merely wishes to know of Yoga rises superior to the performer of Vedic rites.2
